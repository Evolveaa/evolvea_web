A real exercise, in full

"The Stuck Puzzle"

A six-step, parent-led routine that teaches children to stay with difficulty instead of avoiding it. The parent is a facilitator, not an instructor — no correcting, no solving, just reflective prompts and emotional containment.

What it builds

  • Tolerance of cognitive difficulty
  • Adaptive self-talk ("I can try another way")
  • Effort-based, not outcome-based, self-evaluation
  • Parent-mediated scaffolding of problem-solving
  1. 1
    Task presentation

    The child is told they own the thinking. Autonomy first; no evaluative language.

  2. 2
    Emotional awareness

    "How do you feel when you look at this task?" — discomfort is named and normalised.

  3. 3
    Metacognitive planning

    Three questions, plenty of wait time, no answers offered by the parent.

  4. 4
    Task engagement

    The child works independently; frustration is met with prompts, never corrections.

  5. 5
    Reflective evaluation

    "What did you do well? What was hard, and how did you handle it?"

  6. 6
    Self-efficacy anchoring

    The child says a short statement to carry forward: "Being stuck helps me learn."

Communication exercise

The Stuck Puzzle — full protocol

Age 5–10 · ~10 min · parent & child · repeatable with any moderate task

Objectives

  • Increase tolerance of cognitive difficulty
  • Promote adaptive self-talk
  • Reinforce effort-based rather than outcome-based self-evaluation
  • Support parent-mediated scaffolding of problem-solving

Required materials

One age-appropriate task of moderate difficulty — e.g. a small jigsaw puzzle (10–20 pieces). It must be solvable, but not immediately obvious.

Role of the parent

A facilitator, not an instructor. No direct explanation, correction, or solution-giving — only reflective prompts and emotional containment.

The six steps

  1. Task presentation. Present the task; the child owns the thinking process. Emphasise autonomy, avoid evaluative language.
  2. Emotional awareness. "How do you feel when you look at this task?" Offer descriptors if needed (calm, confused, nervous). Acknowledge without judgement.
  3. Metacognitive planning. Ask in sequence, with wait time: "What is this asking you to do?" · "What do you already know that could help?" · "What could be a first small step?"
  4. Task engagement. The child works independently. On frustration, prompt — don't correct: "What could you try next?" · "What did this attempt show you?"
  5. Reflective evaluation. Regardless of completion: "What did you do well?" · "What was difficult, and how did you handle it?" · "What would you try differently?"
  6. Self-efficacy anchoring. The child says a short statement aloud: "I can take this step by step." · "Being stuck helps me learn." · "I can try another way."

Expected outcomes

With repeated use: more confidence with unfamiliar problems, less avoidance of difficulty, sharper reflective awareness, and stronger parent–child learning interactions. Consistency of reflective questioning matters more than the task itself.
